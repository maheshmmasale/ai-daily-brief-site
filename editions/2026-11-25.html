Sakana's 30B model outscores a 120B rival, Cognition's SWE-2.1 turns RL into real exploit-finding skill, and OpenAI's GPT-6 designs proteins in the lab. Plus: DeepMind's agent teams prove faster quantum algorithms, and post-trained open-weight models beat the closed frontier on legal work.

Top Storiescompany claim Sakana AI · 2026-11-25

Sakana Namazu Small

Sakana AI announced Sakana Namazu Small, a 30-billion-parameter reasoning model built on NVIDIA's open Nemotron 3 Nano base model, with continued pretraining and post-training done in-house. Across 25 benchmarks it beat not only the same-size Nemotron 3 Nano but also the roughly four-times-larger Nemotron 3 Super (120B) on average score. The gap is widest in web-search research — BrowseComp 73.5 against the Super's 36.1 — and in long-document understanding with citations. On tasks answered from internal knowledge alone, the gains were small, which Sakana flags openly. On-premises availability details are promised for early December.

Why it matters: A 30B model you can run yourself beating a 120B frontier model on research and document work is the open-weight story in miniature — and the benchmark table is unusually honest about where it doesn't win.

Top Storiescompany claim Cognition · 2026-11-24

Introducing SWE-2.1

Cognition released SWE-2.1, a major upgrade over SWE-2 trained with pure reinforcement learning on Kimi K3, with gains across FrontierCode 2.0 and Terminal-Bench 4.1. The headline numbers are in cybersecurity: 80% on ExploitBench and 27.3% of flags captured via intended exploits on ExploitGym, over hours of autonomous work. Training included red-teaming and a canary eval where the model tries to escape its sandbox — it found new sandbox vulnerabilities four times. Because the capability carries misuse risk, SWE-2.1 ships with safeguards, while a more permissive SWE-2.1 Cyber goes to Devin Security for defensive work. A Cerebras-powered SWE-2.1 Ultrafast runs at 10x speed, and the model is free on Desktop and CLI through December 8.

Why it matters: An agent that finds and chains real exploits for hours, with the lab openly describing its containment canary, is both the capability jump and the safety conversation in one post.

Top Storiescompany claim OpenAI · 2026-11-24

GPT-6 models demonstrate intrinsic protein-design capabilities

OpenAI reports that its GPT-6 family can design new protein structures and amino-acid sequences using only reasoning and general-purpose coding tools, without calling specialized biology models. An internal research model matched or beat specialized models on many of 14 protein tasks. In lab tests with partner Onava, proteins built from GPT-6.2's designs showed evidence of binding across all five targets — 35 of 322 minibinders (10.9%) — and redesigned antibody binding loops kept their target in 70% of 182 designs. On MotifBench scaffolding, the internal model scored 43.7% against 27.8% for the specialized RFdiffusion. Full structure prediction remains a clear weakness.

Why it matters: A general-purpose model doing specialist protein design — and having it wet-lab validated — suggests the frontier is now general reasoning applied to science, not just bigger domain models.

Top Storiescompany claim Google DeepMind · 2026-11-23

Finding faster quantum algorithms with agents and formal verification

DeepMind used teams of Gemini 4 agents to improve well-known quantum algorithms, proving each result with Lean, a formal theorem prover. For inertial confinement fusion, the agents cut the computing cost 11.8x and memory from 5,650 to 4,338 error-corrected qubits. They made quantum simulation of a light-sensitive molecule 8x cheaper, improved Cytochrome P450 simulation 2.5x (481 to 333 qubits), found a 2.57x speedup for a Grover's algorithm implementation, and a 290x speedup for the arcsin routine used to load numbers into quantum states. The agent teams worked independently, then met every four hours in a virtual conference to trade ideas. DeepMind open-sourced its Lean library, problem specifications, and verified solutions.

Why it matters: Agent teams plus machine-checked proofs produced real, measured wins on problems that matter for fusion and drug design — and the whole pipeline is open for others to build on.

Top Storiescompany claim Baseten · 2026-11-24

Open weight models beat the closed frontier on legal tasks with Harvey

Baseten and Harvey post-trained four recently released US open-weight models — Meta's Muse Spark Open, NVIDIA's Nemotron 3.5 Super VL, Reflection's Beam 1.0, and Thinking Machines Lab's Inkling 2 Small — on agentic legal work. On Harvey's Legal Agent Benchmark, Muse Spark Open reached a 30.3% all-pass rate, the first model to clear 30%, and all four open-weight models scored higher than the closed frontier: GPT-6.2 Astra at 16.3% and Claude Fable 5.2 at 5.0%. The gains generalized to contract drafting and review, and the post-trained models showed no regression on the hard subset of SWE-bench Pro. Training ran on Baseten Loops, with rollouts on Baseten Sandboxes.

Why it matters: Post-training a model you own, on your own domain, beating the closed frontier is the strongest practical case yet for open weights in the enterprise — with the numbers to back it.

Frontier Labs & Big Techcompany claim Together AI · 2026-11-24

Introducing preemptible compute: the same compute, half the price

Together AI made preemptible compute generally available on Together GPU Clusters, on Kubernetes and Slurm in all regions. Preemptible nodes run on unused capacity at a flat 50% of the on-demand rate — fixed, not a spot market — with sub-hourly billing metered every one to two minutes. When capacity is reclaimed, workloads get a five-minute drain window (SIGTERM, then removal) and the cluster automatically refills toward its preemptible target. It fits short experiments, evals, batch inference, and bursts that can checkpoint and resume; coordinators and user-facing replicas should stay on standard nodes.

Why it matters: Half-price GPU capacity with a predictable drain window makes the economics of evals, sweeps, and batch inference meaningfully better for anyone training or serving on Together.

Engineering & Researchcompany claim Surge AI · 2026-11-24

Glimmers of RSI: Coding Agents Are on an Exponential Curve

Surge AI tracks coding agents on its sudo L7 staff-level engineering benchmark and finds the curve bending sharply upward: from 8% in 2024 to about 20% by end of 2025 to nearly 65% today. Agents gained 26.5 points in Q3 2026 alone, versus 12 points over the previous nearly two years — the Q3 improvement rate is 20x a year earlier. The post identifies two inflection points: agents learning to get code working by testing and iterating, then developing engineering judgment — challenging assumptions and deciding what should be built. From Q4 2025 to Q3 2026, Verification rose 38 points and Thought Partnership 37. On one task, scores went from 5% to 86% in two years. Even the best agents still fail roughly half of sudo L7 tasks, but Surge argues the shift from code generators to engineering thought partners may mark the beginnings of recursive self-improvement.

Why it matters: The clearest quantitative case that coding agents are not improving linearly — the gains are concentrated in judgment and verification, the exact skills that separate a generator from an engineer.

Engineering & Researchcompany claim Surge AI · 2026-11-24

Better Judgment, Not Just Better Tool Use: What Nemotron Learned From RL

Surge AI reports that reinforcement learning on its long-horizon tool-use data nearly doubled NVIDIA Nemotron 3.5 Super's end-to-end completion on Zapier's AutomationBench, from 14.8% to 27.5% — and from 4.5% to 33.3% on unfamiliar apps. The gains came from judgment, not tool-calling mechanics: finding the rules that govern a task, persisting past dead ends, avoiding fabrication, and respecting policies. It is the companion finding to Surge's harness study: better judgment, learned through RL on realistic long-horizon data, is what moves the needle.

Why it matters: A clean, measured answer to what RL on real tool-use data actually buys — a near-doubling on long-horizon automation, driven by judgment rather than more tool calls.

Engineering & Researchcompany claim LangChain · 2026-11-25

The Agent Lake

LangChain argues that agent traces are becoming a company's most valuable data, and introduces the 'agent lake': storage optimized for traces plus 'intelligent compute' to query and learn from them. LangSmith ingests traces from billions of agent runs per month, stored in SmithDB, its purpose-built trace database. On top sit online evaluators and AI Search for finding high-signal traces, Insights for grouping failures, Engine v2 for diagnosing root causes and proposing fixes as pull requests (60M+ production traces analyzed), and SmithTune for fine-tuning models on the best trajectories — a Kimi K3 fine-tune scored 96 against 90 for the base model on an internal IssueBench subset.

Why it matters: Part vision piece, part product map — but the framing is useful: as agents do more of a company's work, their traces become the training data for the next generation of those same agents.

Engineering & Researchcompany claim Databricks · 2026-11-24

Data Modeling Best Practices for an Agentic CDP, Part 1: The Data Foundation

Databricks lays out how to model customer data for an agentic CDP, where an agent decides for one person in seconds. The prescription: a medallion architecture with identity resolution running after silver, producing one persistent canonical ID per person plus an auditable identity graph; a gold layer of a customer table, 1:1 attribute tables (scores, consent, derived attributes), and 1:many behavior tables at event grain; every gold table flagged batch or event; and engagement outcomes landed back in bronze with holdout groups. The post argues the owned, explainable identity graph is the most important table you will build, and that agents doing the matching still need humans reviewing samples.

Why it matters: A practical, opinionated blueprint for the unglamorous foundation agentic marketing actually depends on — identity and data modeling, not the agent itself.

Microduck Obstacle Avoidance: A Sim2Real Workflow with Isaac Lab and Newton

NVIDIA's tutorial on Hugging Face shows how to add obstacle avoidance to the Microduck robot while keeping its walking policy frozen: a separate navigation controller trained in Isaac Lab adjusts gamepad velocity commands using a time-of-flight sensor modeled with Newton's raycaster (an 8x8 grid at 15Hz). Why it matters: A complete, reproducible sim-to-real recipe — frozen walker, learned avoidance, real hardware — and a good sense of what small-scale robot learning costs in practice.

Worth Your Timecompany claim OpenAI · 2026-11-24

Searching for distant planets with Astra

Two amateur astronomers used OpenAI's tools to hunt for exoplanets in public telescope data. Kevin Bond, an OpenAI engineer, used Codex to search light curves from about 100,000 Kepler stars; the search flagged 200+ signals including 10 already-confirmed planets, and two new candidates — one with a 13.8-day period around a star in Lyra, possibly 2.4 times Earth's diameter. A SETI Institute astronomer independently found two of the same signals. Ethan Byrd used Astra across about 19 million stars, turning up 49 candidates, with one now in line for analysis by NASA's TESS team. Confirmation could take months or years, but the pipeline is a model for citizen science with AI agents.

Why it matters: The most charming read of the week: two people with no telescope time and a lot of curiosity, using agents to do real astronomy on data the professionals had already mined.
