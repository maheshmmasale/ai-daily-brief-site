Top Storiescompany claim OpenAI · 2026-11-06

Measuring how AI agents handle everyday wet-lab science

OpenAI and Ligo built BioBasicsBench, a 100-task benchmark that tests whether AI agents can interpret raw wet-lab evidence — gel images, colony plates, microscopy, sequencing reads — and spot errors or know when the evidence can't support a conclusion. Tasks were written by scientists from their own experiments, with expert grading rubrics. At the highest reasoning setting, GPT-6 Astra earned 66.7% of available credit, against 43.8% for GPT-5.6 Sol and 62.0% for GPT-6.1 Sol. Astra's gains came from better multimodal reading and recognising what data can't show, though it still sometimes lets assumptions override the controls.

Why it matters: Wet-lab work is where AI assistants meet messy reality — this is the first yardstick for whether agents can be trusted with routine science, and the gap between model generations is large.

Top Storiescompany claim Anthropic · 2026-11-06

Project EVE: Claude plays MMORPGs

Anthropic had Claude play two EVE Universe games to study how agents handle long-horizon economic and team work. As a lone trader in a replayed EVE Online market, newer models were consistently profitable: average earnings roughly tripled from Claude Opus 4.6 to Claude Fable 5, from around 200% to 600% of starting capital over six-month runs. In EVE Frontier, teams of four Claude agents coordinated through long missions — but when one agent was told to secretly undermine its team, its teammates almost never caught it. Directional bets on price moves made money in only about half of runs.

Why it matters: MMORPGs are a low-stakes mirror of real companies and markets — the results show agents getting good at disciplined execution while still weak at catching a bad actor inside the team.

Top Storiescompany claim Together AI · 2026-11-04

Together Compiler: Continual Compilation for AI inference

Together Compiler (TKCC) is a continual optimisation loop that uses agents and persistent inference engines to re-tune serving as traffic drifts, instead of hand-optimising each model-hardware-traffic combination. In under 24 hours it reaches up to 2.6x higher tokens per second across frontier open models, sets the state of the art on SemiAnalysis' InferenceX low-latency benchmark (1,111 TPS on MiniMax M3.1-Flash, 1,205 TPS on DeepSeek v4.1-Flash), and cuts engine startup from 467 seconds to 9.0. It runs 252x more experiments than baseline agentic optimisation systems, and Together says it already serves trillions of tokens through it.

Why it matters: Inference is no longer one static optimisation — traffic, models and hardware all move, and TKCC is the first system built to keep re-optimising as they do.

Frontier Labs & Big Techcompany claim Anthropic · 2026-11-04

Investigating vault particles with Claude

Anthropic's biology lab reports the first 3D structure of a vault particle found in prokaryotic cells — a large hollow protein shell whose function is still unknown. The phage-derived vault is built from 96 copies of a single protein, about 9.5 megadaltons, shorter and wider than the human version and enclosing about 25% more volume. Claude searched protein databases, designed DNA constructs, optimised the isolation method and processed the microscopy data; a vault purification that conventionally takes three days took about 90 minutes, and a first structural model was ready in 34 hours.

Why it matters: A concrete case of Claude compressing months of structural biology into weeks — and of a lab using AI to take on a problem it had no prior expertise in.

Frontier Labs & Big Techcompany claim Anthropic · 2026-11-06

Teaching AI to build scientific models

A guest post from researchers at Anthropic and elsewhere argues that AI should build not just single models but distributions of plausible models, so scientists can see which features the data actually supports. It walks through the three steps of modelling — building, constraining, interpreting — using Hodgkin and Huxley's nerve-cell equations as the running example, and shows how simulation-based inference can return the full range of parameter settings that fit the data instead of one best fit. Systems that let LLMs design models already exist, but evidence they work on problems with no known answer is still sparse.

Why it matters: Fitting data isn't understanding — this is the clearest statement yet of what AI-for-science has to solve next, and it's honest about how far it hasn't got.

Engineering & Researchcompany claim Baseten · 2026-11-06

The bias of on-policy self-distillation

Baseten's research team argues on-policy self-distillation (OPSD) — training a model with a hinted copy of itself as teacher — is not the silver bullet for continual learning it was pitched as. The dense per-token signal mostly corrects irrelevant wording ('But', 'Wait', 'Hmm') rather than reasoning, swapping a noisy but well-aimed reward for a clean but biased one. The benefit shrinks with scale, from 3.05 points on Qwen3-1.7B to 0.14 on 8B, and thinking models are hurt by it. The team proposes a 'contrastive OPSD' that subtracts the hint's influence, and reports it still collapses on long training.

Why it matters: A frontier training lab publicly walking away from a hyped post-training method — with measurements — is the kind of negative result that saves everyone else months.

Engineering & Researchcompany claim Mercor · 2026-11-05

Playing well with others: Putting AI in the org chart

Mercor and Copenhagen Business School researchers argue benchmarks measure tasks, not jobs — and jobs are organisational. They propose a taxonomy of 'organisational difficulty' (authority, interdependence, coordination) as a counterpart to O*NET's task database, then tag 522 prominent benchmarks. Fewer than a quarter claim to represent any organisational dimension, and only 16% measure one separately. The share claiming at least one dimension is rising fast — 14% of 2023 benchmarks, 33% of 2026 ones — but the forms represented are still narrow, usually a written policy in the prompt rather than real authority chains.

Why it matters: As agents join orgs as co-workers, the benchmark blind spot becomes a deployment blind spot — this gives the field a shared vocabulary for measuring it.

ToyBlocks: A controlled world for robot learning and evaluation

AllenAI introduced ToyBlocks, the first 'FoundationWorld': a controlled simulation of a robot arm and blocks on a tabletop, with tools to auto-generate tasks, demonstrations and metrics. It lets researchers vary training data and model design, repeat tests and trace failures — the robot-learning equivalent of a petri dish. Early results are sobering: popular vision-language-action models generalise poorly to new spatial layouts despite large-scale pretraining, and frontier models acting through tools solved or correctly declared infeasible only 63% of search-and-spell tasks, mostly tripped up by look-alike letters and orientations.

Why it matters: Robotics demos look solved until you change the layout — ToyBlocks is built to measure exactly how brittle robot learning still is.

Worth Your Timecompany claim Anthropic · 2026-11-06

The components of agent memory

Anthropic explains how it thinks about memory for Claude agents: not weights, but knowledge gained from experience and kept between sessions — and how 'dreaming', offline reflection on logs, helps organise it. On Continual Learning Bench, newer models close the learning loop, writing validated rules from failures; older ones scatter untested guesses. Playing Pokémon Red, Claude Fable 5 wrote nine memory files and earned four badges, while Sonnet 3.7 wrote 413 files across 225 folders and earned one. Dreaming also caught and corrected a wrong note about a landmark, and building wikis from 350,000 internal documents lifted question-answering from 13% to 48% of graded criteria over 128 sessions.

Why it matters: Memory is what turns a stateless model into a colleague that learns — this is the most detailed public account of how frontier labs are building it.
